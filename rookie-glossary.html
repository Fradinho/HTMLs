The Rookie — Cop Jargon Glossary

20 essential terms to follow the radio chatter without a police academy degree

The Rookie drops you straight into LAPD life with zero translation. Officers talk in codes, acronyms, and shorthand that fly by fast. This glossary covers the 20 terms that come up most — bookmark it before your next episode.

  1. 01

    Role Rookie / Boot

    A probationary officer in their first year on the job. "Boot" is the more dismissive street version — rookies have to earn their spurs before veterans stop using it.

  2. 02

    Role TO / FTO — Training Officer

    The experienced officer paired with a rookie to supervise and evaluate them. The TO can end a rookie's career with a bad evaluation, which is why this relationship drives so much of the show's drama.

  3. 03

    Code Code 3

    Respond immediately with lights and sirens. When dispatch calls Code 3, everyone buckles up — it means something serious is unfolding right now.

  4. 04

    Code Code 4

    Scene is secure; no further assistance needed. Officers radio Code 4 to let dispatch know they've handled the situation and backup can stand down.

  5. 05

    Acronym RA — Rescue Ambulance

    The LAPD term for an ambulance unit. Officers radio "roll an RA to my location" whenever someone needs medical attention at a scene. You'll hear it any time someone gets shot, beaten, or collapses — which in The Rookie is often.

  6. 06

    Radio 10-4

    Message received and understood — the universal radio acknowledgement. You'll hear this dozens of times per episode; it's the police version of "OK."

  7. 07

    Acronym EOW — End of Watch

    The end of a shift — but also the solemn term used when an officer dies in the line of duty. The final radio call for a fallen officer is their EOW. The show's title card is a direct nod to this tradition.

  8. 08

    Acronym BOLO — Be On the Look Out

    A broadcast alert to all units describing a suspect or vehicle to watch for. When dispatch puts out a BOLO, every officer on the street starts scanning.

  9. 09

    Acronym APB — All Points Bulletin

    A wider, formal alert broadcast beyond a single district — think region-wide or state-wide notice. More serious than a BOLO and used for high-priority suspects.

  10. 10

    Slang Perp — Perpetrator

    The person who committed the crime. Officers use "perp" as shorthand instead of "suspect" once they're fairly sure of guilt, though legally the two are different.

  11. 11

    Acronym DB — Dead Body

    Exactly what it sounds like. Officers radio in a "DB" when they discover a body at a scene. Clinical and detached by design — emotional distance is a coping mechanism.

  12. 12

    Acronym DOA — Dead on Arrival

    The victim was already dead before paramedics or officers arrived. Used by dispatch and in initial scene reports to set expectations before anyone rolls up.

  13. 13

    Acronym UC — Undercover

    An officer operating in plain clothes without revealing their identity. Going UC is high-risk and high-drama — the show uses it frequently for sting operations and infiltrating criminal organizations.

  14. 14

    Legal MO — Modus Operandi

    Latin for "method of operation" — the specific pattern or technique a criminal uses. Detectives look for a matching MO to link crimes to the same offender.

  15. 15

    Tactic Felony Stop

    A high-risk vehicle stop procedure used when officers believe the occupants are armed or dangerous. Guns drawn, everyone exits with hands visible — you've seen the choreography. Very different from a routine traffic stop.

  16. 16

    Shift Watch

    A patrol shift — morning watch, day watch, or night watch (also called "PM watch"). Officers say they're "on watch" the way most people say they're "at work."

  17. 17

    Shift Roll Call

    The briefing at the start of every watch where the sergeant runs through active BOLOs, neighborhood intelligence, and assignments. Almost every episode opens with one — it's how the writers dump exposition naturally.

  18. 18

    Legal Probable Cause

    The legal threshold an officer must meet to make an arrest or conduct a search without a warrant — more than a hunch, less than certainty. Characters argue over whether they have it constantly.

  19. 19

    Legal Miranda / Mirandize

    Reading a suspect their constitutional rights upon arrest ("You have the right to remain silent…"). To "Mirandize" someone is the verb form. Skipping this step can get evidence thrown out in court — a plot point the show exploits often.

  20. 20

    Hierarchy The Brass

    Senior officers and command staff — captains, commanders, chiefs. "The brass" usually appears when the stakes get political or when someone's career is on the line. They talk about policy; officers on the street talk about survival.
